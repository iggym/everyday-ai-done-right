Step-by-Step Walkthrough

1
Open a Free Google Gemini Session
▼
  1. Go to gemini.google.com
  2. Sign in with any Google account (create one if needed—no payment info required)
  3. Open a new conversation and proceed to Step 2
2
Establish Your Situation Context
▼

Paste this prompt and fill in the brackets with as much or as little detail as you're comfortable sharing:

Situation Context Prompt
I need help drafting accessibility accommodation requests. Here is my situation: Setting: [workplace / university / K-12 school / housing / other] My role: [e.g., "software engineer at a company with 50 employees" / "undergraduate student at a public university"] My disability/condition: [describe in your own words, or use a general description like "a chronic health condition that affects my energy levels" if you prefer not to specify] Specific barriers I'm experiencing: [list 2-5 concrete barriers, e.g., "The open-plan office makes it difficult to concentrate due to sensory overload" / "The online learning platform doesn't work with my screen reader"] Accommodations I believe would help: [list what you have in mind, or say "I'm not sure what to request and need suggestions"] Relevant legal framework: [ADA / Section 504 / FHA / state law / I'm not sure which applies] Do NOT give me legal advice. Instead, confirm you understand my situation and list the general categories of accommodation that are commonly requested in this setting. Do not make claims about what I am or am not legally entitled to.
3
Draft Your Formal Accommodation Request Letter
▼

Once Gemini confirms it understands your context, use this prompt:

Letter Drafting Prompt
Using the situation I described, draft a formal accommodation request letter addressed to [my manager / HR department / disability services office / landlord]. The letter should: 1. Open by stating that I am requesting reasonable accommodations under [the ADA / Section 504 / applicable law] 2. Describe my disability or condition in general terms (I will review and adjust the specificity) 3. Explain how my condition creates barriers in [specific setting/situations] 4. List the specific accommodations I am requesting, with a brief explanation of how each one addresses a barrier 5. State that I am open to discussing alternative accommodations if these are not feasible 6. Include a professional closing and indicate I am available to meet to discuss Tone: Professional, factual, collaborative — not adversarial. The goal is to initiate the "interactive process" constructively. Do NOT include legal citations, case law references, or any language that could be interpreted as legal advice. This is a personal letter, not a legal document.
4
Evaluate a Response or Denial
▼

If you receive a response that feels inadequate, dismissive, or contains concerning language, paste it with this prompt:

Response Evaluation Prompt
I received the following response to my accommodation request. Please analyze it and identify: 1. Whether the response acknowledges the request and engages with the "interactive process" in good faith, or whether it deflects, delays, or dismisses without engagement 2. Any language that could indicate the responder misunderstands their obligations under [ADA/Section 504/applicable law] — note: do NOT tell me what my legal rights are, just flag language patterns that seem inconsistent with general good-faith interactive process principles 3. Specific sentences or phrases that seem problematic and why 4. Suggested language I could use in a follow-up response to redirect the conversation productively Here is the response I received: [PASTE THE RESPONSE HERE] Again: do not give legal advice. Frame everything as "common patterns in the interactive process" and "suggested follow-up language," not as statements about my legal rights.
5
Create a Barrier Documentation Log
▼

If you're encountering ongoing accessibility issues, create a structured record for potential future use:

Barrier Log Template Prompt
Help me create a barrier documentation log. I will describe accessibility barriers I have encountered. For each one, format the entry as: - Date: [I will fill this in] - Location/Context: [I will fill this in] - Barrier Description: [I will fill this in] - Impact: How this barrier affected my ability to [work / learn / access the service / etc.] - Accommodation Requested: [I will fill this in] - Response Received: [I will fill this in] - Status: [Open / Resolved / Escalated] Create a template with 10 blank entries in a table format I can copy into a spreadsheet or document. Include a note at the top reminding me to be factual and specific, avoid emotional language, and include dates and names where possible.

What Breaks (Limitations)

  • Not Legal Advice: Gemini cannot and should not be used as a substitute for a qualified attorney. It can help you draft correspondence and identify response patterns, but it cannot tell you whether your rights have been violated, whether you have a valid claim, or what legal steps to take. If your situation escalates beyond the interactive process, consult a disability rights attorney or your local protection and advocacy agency.
  • Privacy Risk: You are sharing disability-related and potentially medical information with Google's servers. Google's privacy policy governs how this data is used. If this concerns you, use placeholder descriptions in the AI prompts and fill in specifics only in your final, locally-stored documents. Google states it does not use Gemini conversations to train models, but policies can change.
  • Jurisdiction Variability: Accommodation processes differ significantly by country, state, and institution. Gemini may default to U.S.-centric assumptions. Always verify that the process described matches your specific jurisdiction and institution.
  • Institutional Specificity: Every employer, school, and housing provider has its own accommodation procedures. Gemini's letters are starting points—you must adapt them to your institution's specific forms, contacts, and processes.
  • Accuracy of Process Descriptions: While the prompts instruct Gemini to avoid legal claims, it may still describe processes inaccurately or oversimplify them. Cross-reference any process descriptions against official sources (ada.gov, eeoc.gov, your institution's disability services office).
  • Emotional Tone Management: AI-generated letters can sometimes sound too formal, too generic, or oddly phrased. Read the output carefully and rewrite sections in your own voice—your letter should sound like you, not like a machine.

Three Things to Know

1. You don't need to disclose your exact diagnosis +
Under the ADA, you are generally required to disclose that you have a disability and how it affects you, but you do not need to provide a specific diagnosis or detailed medical records at the initial request stage. The prompts above are designed to work with general descriptions. If an employer or school demands more detail than you're comfortable sharing, that may itself be a pattern worth noting—discuss with a disability rights attorney if unsure.
2. The "interactive process" is a legal requirement, not a favor +
Under the ADA and Section 504, covered entities must engage in an interactive process to identify effective accommodations. They can't simply say "no" without discussion. If a response you receive doesn't acknowledge this obligation or refuses to engage, that's a meaningful pattern. The Response Evaluation prompt (Step 4) is specifically designed to help you spot this.
3. Keep everything in writing—starting now +
If you ever need to escalate—internally, to an EEO office, or to an attorney—written records are essential. Use the Barrier Documentation Log (Step 5) from day one, not after problems accumulate. Send accommodation requests via email rather than verbal conversation so you have a timestamped record. If a meeting happens, follow up with an email summarizing what was discussed and agreed upon.
